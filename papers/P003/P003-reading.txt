@task Task 1 · The judgement inside a ranking
@instructions For questions 51–58, choose the correct letter A, B, C or D. Answer according to the passage, which discusses a hypothetical company.
@text
**A** A company is considering a computer system to shortlist job applicants. Its sales representative offers a reassuring demonstration: the programme produces a neat ranking in seconds. The hiring manager, however, asks a less comfortable question. What, exactly, is being ranked? A system can be highly consistent in applying a rule without that rule being appropriate. The appearance of order may settle a discussion before anyone has examined the judgement hidden inside it.

**B** Suppose the system learns from records of people who were hired previously. Those records tell it whom the company selected, not necessarily whom it should have selected. Candidates who were rejected are absent from later performance reviews; nobody knows how they would have performed. Even among employees, opportunities differ. Someone assigned difficult projects may appear less successful than someone protected from them. A training set can therefore contain traces of institutional decisions masquerading as facts about individual ability.

**C** These problems do not establish that a human shortlist would be fairer. An unaided manager can overlook qualified applicants, rely on a familiar school name or change standards without noticing. Replacing a programme with an intuition is not an adequate response to a defective measure. The useful comparison is between specified procedures, each with identifiable limitations. A demand that the new system be flawless while the old one escapes inspection is itself a way of preserving an unexamined judgement.

**D** The company proposes keeping a person in charge of the final decision. This sounds like a safeguard, but its value depends on what that person can do. If the ranking arrives minutes before a deadline and no explanation can be requested, the reviewer may simply endorse it. Accountability cannot be created merely by placing a human signature beneath a recommendation. There must be time to investigate, authority to challenge and a record of why a recommendation was accepted or rejected.

**E** A second safeguard is an appeal. Yet an applicant cannot challenge a mistaken description without knowing that it exists. Nor is an explanation useful if it is so general that it could have been sent to anyone. A meaningful appeal should identify the information used and offer a route for correcting factual mistakes. It need not disclose every technical detail of the software. The point is to give a person something relevant to contest, rather than a ceremonial invitation to disagree.

**F** Testing also needs care. Imagine that the company runs the programme alongside its existing process but allows only the existing process to choose whom to hire. Agreement between the two rankings might be informative. It would not prove that either identifies the best future workers. Both could reproduce the same mistaken assumptions. Disagreement, similarly, is a starting point for investigation, not a verdict against the new method. The company must decide what outcome would count as improvement and what evidence could actually reveal it.

**G** None of this requires abandoning the project. It requires refusing to confuse a purchasing decision with the completion of an evaluation. A tool may save administrative time, help reveal inconsistent criteria or make a process easier to audit. Those are real possibilities, but they are different claims. Before accepting the convenience of a single score, the company should separate them. A ranking is an answer to a question; responsible adoption begins by making sure it is the question worth asking.
@endtext

@task Task 2 · Reading time in timber
@instructions For questions 59–63, choose TRUE if the statement agrees with the passage, FALSE if it contradicts the passage, or NOT GIVEN if the passage does not provide the information. For questions 64–67, write NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage.
@text
**A** A tree’s rings can provide more than a rough guide to its age. In the climates described here, growth changes during the season. Earlywood forms when growth is faster; latewood forms as growth slows. These contrasting layers together make one annual ring. Counting both layers separately would therefore count a single year twice.

**B** Ring widths vary with growing conditions. A broad ring can reflect favourable conditions, while a narrow one may indicate stress. Moisture and temperature matter, but they are not the only influences: insects, fire and competition from nearby trees can affect growth too. A narrow ring is consequently a record requiring interpretation, not an automatic label for drought.

**C** Researchers can extract a thin core rather than remove a complete section of a trunk. The sample provides a sequence of rings while limiting the amount of material taken. Samples from trees that grew in the same region may share distinctive patterns of wide and narrow rings. Matching these patterns is called crossdating. Overlapping sequences from living trees, dead trees and older timber allow a chronology to extend beyond the lifespan of any one living tree.

**D** The method can also help date wooden structures. Once a beam’s rings have been matched to a chronology, its outermost surviving ring gives an important clue to when the tree stopped growing. At Aztec Ruins, researchers have used beams to investigate building activity and later replacement of damaged wood. The evidence has helped distinguish construction phases rather than treating the surviving structure as the product of a single moment.

**E** Interpretation still matters. The date of the last surviving ring is evidence about wood, and a claim about a building must connect that evidence with the wood’s use. For example, if timber had been stored or reused before entering a structure, equating the tree’s final year with the building’s first year would be unjustified. This conditional example does not show that every beam was reused. It shows why a precise measurement and a justified historical conclusion are not quite the same achievement.
@endtext
@note Source: adapted from National Park Service, "Dendrochronology: The Study of Tree Rings" (D. Eakin & E. Horton, 2019) and Aztec Ruins National Monument, "Dendrochronology" (2022).

@text
I had been invited to write a catalogue entry for a small exhibition of paintings. The curator sent me images that were sharp enough to reveal each crack in the varnish. I felt unusually well prepared. By the time I arrived at the store room, I had already drafted a confident paragraph about the restraint of one artist’s colours.

[68]

The conservator watched me compare the image on my tablet with the object on the table. She did not tell me which view was correct. Instead, she moved a lamp. A surface that had seemed uniformly brown began to separate into areas of uneven gloss. My confident adjective, restrained, was starting to look less like a description than an excuse for not looking closely.

[69]

I asked whether cleaning would reveal what the artist had really intended. She paused at the word really. There were records of earlier treatment, but the records did not explain every alteration. Some changes belonged to the object’s history; others prevented people from seeing the work. A decision to intervene required more than an attractive account of what might be underneath.

[70]

She showed me a small test area beside a part that had not been treated. The contrast was striking. I began imagining the whole picture transformed. She asked me to look again at the edge of the test. The result was evidence about that location, she said, not permission to extrapolate across the entire surface.

[71]

That distinction followed me back to my desk. I had been asked for a short entry, and short entries prefer clean stories. An artist chooses a colour; time conceals it; an expert restores it. The sequence offered a satisfying arc. It also made uncertainty look like a temporary obstacle that expertise would eventually remove.

[72]

The curator was willing to include a sentence about the treatment record. She was less sure about my proposal to describe the limits of the evidence. Would that leave the reader with nothing to hold on to? I understood the worry. A label that merely announced our ignorance would be as unhelpful as one that pretended to know everything.

[73]

In the final entry, I kept the observation that the surface had changed, identified the evidence for it and stopped short of reconstructing an original appearance. The result was less dramatic than my first draft. It was also a description I could imagine revising without having to conceal the fact that I had revised it.
