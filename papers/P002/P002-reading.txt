@task Task 1 · What a replica can preserve
@instructions For questions 1–8, choose A, B, C or D.
@text
[1] A museum can make a remarkably convincing copy of an object and still fail to reproduce what matters about it. This is not a complaint about inaccurate colour or a slightly wrong shape. Those defects can sometimes be corrected. The harder question concerns the kind of relationship a visitor is being offered. To see a surface that resembles an ancient bowl is not quite the same experience as encountering a bowl that somebody once held. A replica and an original can look alike while making different claims on our attention.

[2] That distinction need not amount to an argument against copies. If a fragile bowl must remain behind glass, a well-made replica could allow visitors to explore its weight, balance and handle. For someone who cannot examine the display visually, that opportunity may be particularly important. Here the copy is valuable because it provides an experience that the museum cannot responsibly offer with the original. It is not pretending to preserve every aspect of the original encounter.

[3] Trouble begins when visual accuracy becomes the only test of success. A digital model may reproduce the geometry of a damaged surface, yet omit the explanation of why the damage matters. Was a mark made during manufacture, through everyday use, or by a later attempt at repair? The model does not answer merely by becoming more detailed. Interpretation involves deciding which features deserve notice and connecting them with evidence. More information and more understanding are related ambitions, not interchangeable achievements.

[4] There is also a difference between keeping a file and preserving a usable resource. A model stored in a format that no available software can open is not made accessible by the fact that its bits remain intact. Someone must maintain descriptions, check copies and plan for technological change. This work attracts less attention than the moment of scanning, but it is part of what gives the scan a future. An institution that budgets only for acquisition has not yet budgeted for stewardship.

[5] A replica may nevertheless take pressure off an object that would otherwise travel repeatedly. Several museums could display copies while the original remained in stable conditions. That possibility should be weighed against what audiences lose when all contact is mediated by reproduction. The choice need not be between unrestricted travel and permanent disappearance into storage. Carefully limited loans, local access and clearly labelled copies may serve different purposes within the same collection.

[6] Honest labelling is therefore more than a technical nicety. It tells visitors what sort of evidence stands before them. A copy need not apologise for being a copy, but it should not borrow the authority of age through ambiguity. The most useful question is not whether a replica is as good as the original in every respect. It is which experiences it makes possible, which claims it can support, and which losses remain. A convincing substitute becomes an educational resource when its limits, as well as its achievements, are made intelligible.
@endtext

@task Task 2 · What a playground is for
@instructions For questions 9–13, choose Y if the statement agrees with the writer’s view or information, N if it contradicts it, or NG if it is not given. For questions 14–18, choose A, B, C or D.
@text
[1] Visit a newly built playground in many towns and you will find much the same design: low platforms, rounded corners, a soft surface underfoot and equipment that can be used in only one way. Each feature has a sensible origin. Taken together, however, they suggest that the main purpose of a playground is to prevent accidents. That aim deserves respect, but it is not the same as asking what children go there to do.

[2] Children seldom treat a climbing frame as a piece of exercise equipment. They test how high they dare to go, invent rules with friends and change those rules when a game stops working. In doing so they practise judging their own abilities, which is difficult to learn from instruction alone. A structure that offers no uncertainty may be safe to use, but it leaves this judgement with nothing to work on.

[3] It helps to separate two ideas that everyday speech often merges. A risk is a challenge that a child can see and choose to accept or refuse, such as a higher branch or a wider gap. A hazard is a danger the child cannot reasonably detect: a loose bolt, a sharp edge hidden under paint, a space in which a head could become trapped. Designers have every reason to remove hazards. Removing every risk is a different decision, and it should be argued for rather than assumed.

[4] Why, then, does the cautious design keep returning? Part of the answer lies in who notices what. An injury produces a report, a complaint and possibly a claim for compensation. A child who grows bored and stops coming produces nothing at all. Those responsible for a playground are therefore far more likely to hear about the harm they failed to prevent than about the opportunity they quietly removed.

[5] None of this means that adults should step back. Younger children need equipment matched to their size, and supervision matters most where a fall could be serious. Nor should a deliberate challenge be confused with neglect: a frame that is poorly maintained offers danger, not adventure. What is needed is a clearer account of why each feature is there, so that a challenge is kept on purpose and a hazard is removed on purpose.

[6] A good playground, in other words, is not one where nothing can go wrong. It is one where children meet difficulties they can understand, attempt and sometimes fail at without lasting harm. Judging a playground only by the absence of accidents may reassure the people who manage it, yet it can overlook what the space was meant to offer. Safety is a condition for play; it should not become a substitute for it.
