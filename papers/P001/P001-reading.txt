@task Task 1 · Counting what counts
@instructions Read the discussion of a hypothetical library dashboard. For questions 1–8, choose A, B, C or D.
@text
[1] A town library is considering a new way to assess its work. In this hypothetical example, the council wants a dashboard showing book loans, visits and attendance at events. These are sensible things to count. They are also convenient: the lending system already records loans, a counter can register entries, and an organiser can count chairs. The difficulty begins when the figures are asked to stand for the whole purpose of the library. A service can become easier to describe while becoming harder to understand.

[2] Consider two afternoon events. One fills a room with people who already use the library every week. The other attracts a handful of new visitors who have never felt comfortable entering it before. Attendance alone would favour the first. That does not make the first event a failure, or the second an automatic triumph. It means that the decision depends on what the events were intended to achieve. Counting before defining the purpose can produce an impressively precise answer to a question nobody has agreed to ask.

[3] The obvious remedy is to add more indicators. The council might record new registrations, returning visitors and comments from participants. Each would reveal something the attendance total misses. Yet more numbers do not necessarily mean a fuller picture. If staff are rewarded for new registrations, they might concentrate on signing people up rather than helping them use the service. A measure that once supplied useful evidence can become an instruction about what deserves effort. This risk does not require dishonest employees; people can respond quite conscientiously to the incentives they are given.

[4] Nor can a collection of grateful comments settle the matter. People who write to the library may be unusually enthusiastic, while people who quietly stop coming leave no explanation. The comments can still illuminate what the service means to particular users. Their limitation concerns the range of experiences they represent, not whether those experiences are real. A moving story should not be made to do the work of a representative survey, any more than a survey average should be expected to convey the texture of one person’s experience.

[5] A better dashboard would therefore include a short account of what its figures can and cannot support. If visits rose after opening hours were extended, that sequence would be worth reporting. It would not by itself establish that the extension caused the increase: a school nearby might have changed its timetable at the same time. Asking staff to discuss such possibilities need not be an excuse for doing nothing. It can identify what evidence to collect next and which decisions are safe enough to try on a limited scale.

[6] The point is not that judgment should replace measurement. Unexamined intuition can be as self-serving as an unexamined target. The stronger arrangement makes each answerable to the other: figures can challenge a manager’s favourite story, and a well-supported explanation can challenge what a figure appears to show. The dashboard should begin a conversation about public value, not bring that conversation to a ceremonious end. Precision earns its authority when we know what it is precise about.
@endtext

@task Task 2 · A place to be quiet
@instructions For questions 9–13, choose the paragraph (A–F) that contains the idea. Use each letter once; ONE paragraph is extra. For questions 14–18, choose A, B, C or D.
@text
A. A public reading room can offer something that a private subscription cannot: somewhere to remain without being expected to buy another drink. Its value is not confined to the books on the shelves. For a person living in a crowded home, a chair and a stretch of uninterrupted time may be the most important resources in the building. Quiet, in this setting, is an activity made possible by collective arrangements rather than an absence that simply happens.

B. This does not mean a room can satisfy everyone merely by displaying a silence sign. A student may need to discuss an assignment; another visitor may find even a whispered exchange distracting. Both uses can be reasonable. The apparent conflict is sometimes better addressed by providing different spaces or different periods than by declaring that one kind of user is the authentic reader and the other an intruder. The rule should serve a purpose that can be explained.

C. There are nevertheless reasons to resist the demand that every part of a library become conversational. Someone who wants a discussion can often ask others to join one. Someone who needs silence cannot create it independently while remaining in a noisy room. The ability to leave is not equivalent to an alternative place to go. A policy that treats all preferences as equally easy to exercise may therefore distribute its costs very unequally.

D. An online reservation system may seem an elegant solution to disputes about seats. It removes the uncertainty of arriving to find every desk occupied. But it also changes the nature of entry: visitors must plan, have access to the system and know how to use it. A few unreserved desks can preserve the possibility of an unplanned visit. Efficiency in allocating a resource is not the only consideration when part of that resource’s value lies in being readily available.

E. Staff also have to enforce whatever arrangements are chosen. If a policy relies on a librarian endlessly asking people to be quieter, the design has transferred a persistent conflict from the room to the employee. A booking confirmation could explain the quiet area, while signs could direct groups towards a discussion room. Such measures do not eliminate disagreement; they prevent the person at the desk from having to invent the policy anew in every encounter.

F. None of this requires making quiet sacred. A rule which once protected concentration can outlive the circumstances that justified it. The useful question is what would be lost if the rule disappeared, who would bear that loss, and whether the same purpose could be served less restrictively. Public quiet is defensible because it enables people to do things, not because quiet people are more virtuous than everybody else. Its defence is strongest when it can survive that distinction.
