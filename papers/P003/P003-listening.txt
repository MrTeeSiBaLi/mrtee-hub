@task Task 1 · A bakery changes its plans
@audio P003-TASK1.mp3 | Recording 1
@transcript Show transcript (listen first, then check)
**Interviewer:** You used to sell everything over the counter, Mina. Why did you introduce online orders?

**Mina:** People assume I wanted a bigger market. In fact, the shop was already as busy as we could manage. What wore me down was deciding how much to bake. On wet Tuesdays we threw bread away; on sunny ones we apologised to people who had come a long way. Advance orders gave us something firmer to work with. They did not make the uncertainty disappear, but they reduced it.

**Interviewer:** Did customers welcome the idea?

**Mina:** Some did. Others thought you would need an account and a complicated password just to buy a loaf. Our first design rather justified their fears. We asked for details we did not need. So we cut the form back to a name, a contact number and the order. We still take orders by phone, and nobody has to use the website to shop here.

**Interviewer:** Your collection window was originally just half an hour. Was that to save on wages?

**Mina:** No, it was because I thought a short window would make the handover efficient. It did, for us. For someone whose bus ran late, it was a different matter. We widened it before anyone actually lost an order. It was a reminder that efficiency depends on whose time you count.

**Interviewer:** I noticed you no longer call the service a subscription.

**Mina:** That word made people expect a regular payment and a parcel selected for them. Neither was involved. They choose each item and pay only when they place an order. Changing the name solved a misunderstanding that another page of instructions would probably have made worse.

**Interviewer:** Did you measure whether the new system cut waste?

**Mina:** We compared the number of unsold loaves before and after it started. That number fell. But we also reduced the range of bread during the same period, so I cannot give the ordering system all the credit. I would like to test the two changes separately when we have a quieter month. For now, the numbers are encouraging, rather than conclusive.

**Interviewer:** What about your plan to offer collection from other shops?

**Mina:** One nearby shop is interested. I am tempted, but the difficulty is not persuading people to buy. It is making sure a loaf reaches the right counter at the right time. If we cannot do that reliably, a wider reach just gives us more places to disappoint people. We will run a small trial before announcing anything permanent.

**Interviewer:** So the system has made you more cautious?

**Mina:** More specific, perhaps. I used to ask whether an idea sounded promising. Now I ask what would have to be true for it to work, and what evidence would persuade me that it was not working. That is a less exciting conversation, but a more useful one.
@endtranscript
@instructions For questions 1–6, choose the correct letter A, B, C or D.
1. Why did Mina introduce advance orders?
A. To attract customers from a wider area
B. To replace the physical shop
C. To plan production with less uncertainty
D. To ease pressure on a crowded shop
ANS: C
WHY: Her real problem was "deciding how much to bake". Advance orders "gave us something firmer to work with" – they reduced uncertainty. A is wrong: she says people only _assume_ she wanted a bigger market. B is wrong: the shop still sells and nobody has to use the website. D is a trap: the shop was "already as busy as we could manage", but she did not start orders to make it less busy.
2. What did Mina change after seeing the first website design?
A. The amount of information requested
B. The availability of telephone ordering
C. The requirement to register before visiting
D. The prices shown for individual products
ANS: A
WHY: "We asked for details we did not need. So we cut the form back to a name, a contact number and the order." She asked for less information. B is wrong: "We still take orders by phone." C is wrong: nobody has to use the website. D: prices are never mentioned.
3. Why was the collection window extended?
A. Customers had already lost several orders
B. The original arrangement was too expensive
C. The short window slowed down the handover
D. The original arrangement overlooked customers' circumstances
ANS: D
WHY: The short window worked for the shop, but "For someone whose bus ran late, it was a different matter." It did not think about customers' situations. A is wrong: they widened it "before anyone actually lost an order". B is wrong: she says it was not to save on wages. C is the opposite: the short window did make the handover efficient ("It did, for us").
4. The word "subscription" was removed because it
A. suggested that the bread was unusually costly
B. created false expectations about the service
C. prevented customers from choosing individual items
D. had to be supported by more detailed instructions
ANS: B
WHY: The word "made people expect a regular payment and a parcel selected for them. Neither was involved." People expected something that was not true. A: cost is not mentioned. C: customers do choose each item. D is the opposite: changing the name solved the problem; more instructions "would probably have made worse".
5. What is Mina's reservation about the waste figures?
A. They show no improvement in unsold stock
B. They do not isolate the effect of advance ordering
C. They lack a record from before the ordering began
D. They were collected during an unusually quiet month
ANS: B
WHY: "We also reduced the range of bread during the same period, so I cannot give the ordering system all the credit." Two changes happened together, so she cannot see the effect of ordering alone. A is wrong: the number of unsold loaves fell. C is wrong: they compared "before and after". D is a trap: "a quieter month" is when she wants to test in the future.
6. Mina's final response presents caution as
A. accepting that promising ideas cannot be evaluated
B. prioritising operational convenience over demand
C. trusting customers' enthusiasm for promising ideas
D. making the assumptions behind a proposal testable
ANS: D
WHY: She now asks "what would have to be true for it to work, and what evidence would persuade me that it was not working". She makes her assumptions clear so they can be checked. A is the opposite: she wants to evaluate ideas. B comes from an earlier answer about delivery, not her definition of caution. C is her OLD way of thinking ("whether an idea sounded promising").

**Speaker 1:** A campaign group sent us a photograph of a flooded street. It was dramatic, and we were about to use it. Then a colleague noticed that a shop sign belonged to a business that had closed years earlier. The photograph was genuine, but it was not from the storm we were covering. The group had forwarded it in good faith. We found a current image, and since then I have asked where and when a picture was made before asking how powerfully it tells the story.

**Speaker 2:** We reported that the council had spent a certain sum on new benches. The figure was wrong: it included lighting as well. Once the council pointed that out, we fixed the online story. At first we were going to say nothing about the change. Then we realised that readers who had already shared the story would have no way of knowing. We added a correction explaining both the original mistake and the new figure. Making the page accurate was only part of repairing the record.

**Speaker 3:** My headline said a new café would create twenty jobs. In the interview, the owner had actually said he hoped eventually to employ that many people. The number had survived editing; the uncertainty had not. Nobody had invented a figure, which made the error easy to defend as mere shorthand. But the headline had turned an aspiration into an announcement. I rewrote it. Accuracy can disappear without a single noun or number being false in isolation.

**Speaker 4:** A resident wanted us to publish every email in a dispute over a noisy delivery yard. Some messages included private family details. They might have made the story more absorbing, but they did not help explain whether the noise rules had been followed. We kept the relevant exchanges and left those details out. The fact that someone is willing to give you material does not settle whether readers need to see all of it.

**Speaker 5:** Two organisations gave us sharply different estimates of attendance at a street event. We could have presented both figures and called that balance. Instead, we asked how each count had been made. One included everybody passing through the area over six hours; the other was a count at a single moment. Publishing them as rival answers to one question would have manufactured a disagreement. We explained the two methods before using either number.

@task Task 4 · A coastal path survey
@audio P003-TASK4.mp3 | Recording 4
@transcript Show transcript (listen first, then check)
**Coordinator:** Before we go out, let me explain how our path survey works. For today’s training exercise, please use the route map supplied today rather than a map you happen to find online. Our purpose is to record access problems, not to produce a scenic guide. A spectacular view can wait; a barrier that prevents someone completing the route cannot.

**Coordinator:** You will work in pairs. One person makes the measurements and the other writes them down, but change roles at the halfway point. That gives each of you experience of both jobs. Do not split up to cover more ground: if you disagree about what you are looking at, you need to discuss it while you are both there.

**Coordinator:** Every route has a letter. Put that route letter at the top of each sheet. Write the starting time as well, because conditions can change. For the path surface, we use three categories: firm, loose and muddy. Please do not invent extra categories. If none seems adequate, write a comment underneath the table. It is easier to interpret a few agreed terms with comments than twenty private systems that sound more precise.

**Coordinator:** Use the tape measure for gaps between posts or gates. Measure the narrowest point that a person would have to pass through, rather than the widest opening you can find. If a gate swings into the gap, include that in your description. A single width measurement will not tell the whole story, so a brief sketch may help.

**Coordinator:** Take photographs of barriers, but avoid including people who have not agreed to appear. Each photograph needs the number of the matching record on your sheet. A collection of images without that link can be attractive and almost useless. If you cannot take a photograph safely from the path, leave it. Recording a hazard is not a reason to step into one.

**Coordinator:** At the stream crossing, judge the approach from the marked viewing point. After heavy rain, a section may be unsafe even if it looked manageable last week. You have no instruction to cross the stream. If you cannot continue, record the last safe point and return by the same route. An incomplete survey that explains its own limits is more useful than a complete one that puts someone at risk.

**Coordinator:** When you reach the centre, do not immediately combine the two observers’ sheets into a single neat version. Keep the original sheets. We want to know where descriptions differed as well as where they agreed. You can add a joint note explaining the difference. We are mapping obstacles in the landscape, but we are also checking which instructions produce consistent observations.

**Coordinator:** Finally, please hand everything in at the equipment desk, not at reception. Return the tape measure there too. If you have an unfinished record, mark it incomplete and explain why. We would rather know what you did not establish than fill a blank with an assumption.
@endtranscript
